## Multiple-choice explanations

**1. M17-Q01** — correct answer **B: Written authorisation and the actions explicitly out of scope**

Rules of engagement are the authority document: who may test what, when, and what is forbidden, including actions that could affect real users.

- **A** — The attack list is the plan, which sits under the authority.
- **C** — Environment details appear in both, but they do not define authorisation.
- **D** — Timelines are planning content.

**2. M17-Q02** — correct answer **A: It maps threats to components so gaps are visible, and payloads become instances rather than the goal**

A list measures how many strings were tried; a matrix measures which threat-by-component cells were exercised, which is what a reader needs to judge coverage.

- **B** — Case count is not the advantage.
- **C** — Success measurement remains essential.
- **D** — No method guarantees completeness.

**3. M17-Q03** — correct answer **D: The exact prompt, model version, sampling parameters and observed output**

Reproduction needs the inputs and configuration, because sampling and model version change outputs; without them the finding is an anecdote.

- **A** — A screenshot shows an outcome without the conditions.
- **B** — Severity is an assessment, not a reproduction artefact.
- **C** — Intent is not evidence.

**4. M17-Q04** — correct answer **C: From impact and exploitability in this deployment, with the assumption stated**

Impact and exploitability are what a decision-maker needs; sophistication and payload counts describe the test, not the risk.

- **A** — Sophistication is not consequence.
- **B** — Payload counts measure the test, not the exposure.
- **D** — Age is unrelated to severity.

**5. M17-Q05** — correct answer **B: The capability reached, the reproduction steps and the control that would prevent it**

Fixability requires knowing what the attacker gained, how to reproduce it and which control addresses the mechanism rather than the phrasing.

- **A** — Architecture detail rarely changes the fix.
- **C** — Opinion is not evidence.
- **D** — The full payload list is appendicial, not the core of the finding.

**6. M17-Q06** — correct answer **A: Stop the action, notify the contact named in the rules of engagement and preserve what happened**

Containment and notification are the agreed procedure; continuing converts an authorised test into an incident, and silence removes the other party's ability to respond.

- **B** — Evidence matters, but not at the cost of ongoing harm.
- **C** — Deletion destroys evidence and hides a reportable event.
- **D** — Increasing rate amplifies the harm.

**7. M17-Q07** — correct answer **D: Threat family and system component**

Threat by component shows which combinations were exercised, such as injection against retrieval or extraction against the API, and makes gaps obvious.

- **A** — Length and language are payload properties, not coverage axes.
- **B** — Size and latency describe performance.
- **C** — Tester and date are logistics.

**8. M17-Q08** — correct answer **C: Because an unreproduced observation cannot be fixed or accepted with confidence**

The distinction tells the reader what is established versus suspected; mixing them lets speculation inherit the credibility of evidence.

- **A** — Counting is not the purpose.
- **B** — Attempts may be real but need reproduction.
- **D** — Method disclosure is the opposite of the intent.

**9. M17-Q09** — correct answer **B: Re-running the original test with the same conditions and recording the new result**

A retest repeats the original conditions so the comparison is valid; a new suite measures something else and cannot show the finding is closed.

- **A** — A different suite answers a different question.
- **C** — Self-report is not verification.
- **D** — Delay allows regressions and stale evidence.

**10. M17-Q10** — correct answer **A: Testers may see personal data, credentials or model internals that must not be retained or reused**

Testing deliberately reaches sensitive paths, so retention, deletion and permitted use of what is observed must be agreed before the test begins.

- **B** — Speed is not the purpose.
- **C** — Authorisation remains required.
- **D** — The clause does not affect findings.

## Written model answers

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-s1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-S1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Name three items that must appear in the rules of engagement for an LLM test and why each matters.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The named authorising person and the exact systems in scope, because authorisation is specific. The prohibited actions, such as causing outbound messages or touching production data, because harm during a test is still harm. The stop and notification procedure, because an unexpected effect must reach the client immediately rather than at report time.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-s2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-S2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">State the difference between a finding and an observation in a red-team report.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A finding is a reproduced behaviour with evidence, a stated impact and a control that would address it. An observation is something noticed but not reproduced, recorded separately so it can be investigated without being treated as established.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-c1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-C1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain why attack success rate alone is an incomplete red-team metric, and what to report alongside it.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A success rate depends on the payload set, the number of attempts and the definition of success, so it is comparable only within a fixed configuration and can be lowered by testing weaker attacks. Report the configuration, the success criterion per capability, the number of attempts, and a measure of detection: whether the organisation's monitoring saw the activity. A test that succeeds but is detected and contained has a different risk than one that succeeds silently, and only the second pair of numbers shows that.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-c2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-C2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A client asks for a red-team exercise two weeks before launch with no time to fix anything. What do you propose and why?</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Propose a scoped assessment that targets the highest-consequence paths: the capabilities with external effects, the data boundaries and the authentication surface, with a rapid report of blocking issues rather than a comprehensive matrix. The reason is that a broad test with no remediation window produces a document nobody can act on, while a narrow test can still stop a launch that exposes customer data. State explicitly what was not covered, so the client's acceptance decision is informed rather than reassured.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-sc1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-SC1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Write the scope section for a red-team engagement against a customer-support assistant with a refund tool and a knowledge base.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>In scope: the assistant's chat interface, the retrieval index in a staging copy, the refund tool in sandbox mode, and the authentication layer, using two test accounts. Out of scope: production customer data, real payment rails, denial-of-service testing, and social engineering of staff. Constraints: no outbound email to external addresses, refunds capped at a nominal amount, and all activity logged with a shared test marker so defenders can deconflict. Deliverables and the emergency contact are named, and the client signs before any testing begins.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-sc2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-SC2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A finding shows that a retrieved document can make the assistant call the refund tool for an arbitrary order. Write the finding in a form an engineering team can action.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Title: indirect injection reaches refund execution. Evidence: the injected document text, the query that retrieved it, the tool call arguments and the model version with sampling settings. Impact: an unauthorised refund for any order id known to the attacker, bounded only by the tool's cap. Reproduction: step-by-step with the staging accounts. Recommendation: authorise refunds against the requesting customer's orders, require confirmation above a threshold, and treat retrieved text as data. Severity: high, because the effect is financial and requires no privileged access.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-p1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-P1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Practical programming · 8 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Build a six-cell coverage matrix over threat families and components, run one synthetic test per cell, and produce a table with reproduced, evidence, control and owner.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A complete implementation defines cells such as injection against the prompt, cross-tenant retrieval against the index, and tampering against the artefact, then executes a small check per cell that returns whether the behaviour reproduced with a one-line evidence string. The table carries severity, a named control and an owner, and a coverage statement lists what was not tested for each cell. Assertions should require that every cell names a control and that at least a majority reproduce, so the exercise demonstrates both findings and gaps rather than only success.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-ct1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-CT1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A vendor publishes a red-team report with a single 'no critical findings' line. Assess its value.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The line reports an outcome without the scope, method or evidence needed to interpret it, so it cannot be distinguished from a shallow test. A useful report names the systems and versions tested, the threat-by-component coverage, the attacks and their configuration, the success criteria, and the findings with severity and remediation status. It also states what was out of scope, because 'no critical findings' within a narrow scope is compatible with a serious exposure just outside it. Without those elements the claim is marketing rather than assurance.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m17-ct2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M17-CT2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">You find a serious vulnerability in a vendor's product during an authorised test of your own deployment. What are your obligations and limits?</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Notify the vendor promptly through a named security contact, with enough detail to reproduce and a proposed disclosure timeline, and inform your own organisation because the exposure may affect other deployments. Do not publish exploit detail before a fix or an agreed date, and do not test other customers' instances, since your authorisation covers your deployment only. Preserve evidence, avoid accessing data beyond what is needed to demonstrate the issue, and record the coordination so the decision to disclose is documented rather than improvised.</p></div>
