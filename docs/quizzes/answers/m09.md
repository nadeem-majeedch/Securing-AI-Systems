## Multiple-choice explanations

**1. M09-Q01** — correct answer **C: The true-positive rate at a fixed low false-positive rate**

Membership inference is a ranking problem under an error constraint; reporting TPR at a stated FPR, or the advantage over that FPR, is what makes two attacks comparable.

- **A** — Accuracy at a fixed threshold mixes both error types and hides the operating point.
- **B** — Shadow-model count is a method detail, not a result.
- **D** — Model size is an input to leakage, not a measure of it.

**2. M09-Q02** — correct answer **B: Individual training rows are recoverable through output behaviour**

Perfect fit means the model's response encodes each row's peculiarities, which is exactly the signal a membership or inversion attack exploits.

- **A** — Perfect training accuracy says nothing about generalisation and usually suggests the opposite.
- **C** — Extractability depends on query access and output granularity.
- **D** — Differential privacy is a training guarantee, not a consequence of fitting.

**3. M09-Q03** — correct answer **A: Attribute inference estimates a property; inversion reconstructs a representative input**

Attribute inference predicts a sensitive property, often from a score, while inversion optimises an input to maximise a class probability and returns a prototype rather than a person.

- **B** — Both can be run with query access alone.
- **C** — Both apply to classifiers and regressors.
- **D** — They have different objectives and different outputs.

**4. M09-Q04** — correct answer **D: How much any single training record can change the model's output distribution**

Differential privacy bounds per-record influence with a stated epsilon, which is why it also bounds membership leakage; composition across queries is a separate accounting step.

- **A** — Query limits are policy, not the guarantee.
- **B** — Encryption protects storage, not output distributions.
- **C** — Accuracy loss is a cost of the mechanism, not what it bounds.

**5. M09-Q05** — correct answer **C: Returning a coarse band instead of a probability**

Output granularity removes the fine-grained signal attacks rely on; a three-band response carries a fraction of the information of a probability.

- **A** — More capacity typically increases memorisation.
- **B** — A notice changes expectations, not the information returned.
- **D** — Logging supports detection after the fact, not prevention.

**6. M09-Q06** — correct answer **B: The model leaks a strong ranking signal but the useful operating point is constrained**

The two metrics answer different questions: the ranking is informative across the score range, while at a strict false-positive budget the attacker gains little; both belong in the report.

- **A** — A small advantage at one operating point does not erase the ranking signal.
- **C** — The metrics are consistent: they measure different regions of the ROC curve.
- **D** — Differential privacy is a training guarantee, not an inference from these numbers.

**7. M09-Q07** — correct answer **A: They provide labelled examples of how a similar model behaves on members and non-members**

The attacker trains models on data they control, so they know the ground-truth membership and can fit a classifier that maps confidence to membership.

- **B** — Shadow models approximate behaviour; they do not expose weights.
- **C** — Queries are still needed against the target.
- **D** — Determinism is unrelated to the shadow-model technique.

**8. M09-Q08** — correct answer **D: The optimiser overshoots the prototype because it maximises probability without a data prior**

An unconstrained search moves to the extreme of the score surface; the recovered object is a direction, which is why inversion is scored by similarity to a prototype rather than by exact reconstruction.

- **A** — Inversion recovers prototypes, not individuals.
- **B** — Poisoning is unrelated to the overshoot.
- **C** — The model is being used as a classifier throughout.

**9. M09-Q09** — correct answer **C: No leakage was detected by this attack at this operating point on this population**

A negative result is scoped by method, operating point and population; claiming privacy in general converts an absence of evidence into evidence of absence.

- **A** — Privacy is a property of the whole system, not a conclusion from one test.
- **B** — A stronger attack or different population may still succeed.
- **D** — The test says nothing about what the data contains.

**10. M09-Q10** — correct answer **B: Less capacity or more noise, in exchange for accuracy**

Reducing memorisation or adding calibrated noise lowers leakage and lowers accuracy; the design task is to find the point where the remaining leakage is acceptable.

- **A** — Both increase memorisation.
- **C** — Neither addresses the trade.
- **D** — Key length is unrelated to model leakage.

Report the true-positive rate at a stated false-positive rate, or equivalently the advantage over that FPR, because it fixes the error the attacker must tolerate.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-s2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-S2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain in two sentences why a model's confidence is a privacy signal.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A model tends to be more confident and more correct on records it fitted during training than on records it did not, because it encoded their idiosyncrasies. That systematic difference in confidence is what an attacker thresholds to rank members above non-members.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-c1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-C1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain the relationship between overfitting, memorisation and membership leakage, and why a low test loss does not settle the question.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Overfitting means the model fits idiosyncrasies of individual training records; memorisation is the extreme case where a record's information is encoded almost uniquely. Membership leakage exploits the resulting difference between behaviour on seen and unseen records. A low test loss shows the model generalises on average, which reduces but does not eliminate that difference: leakage can persist in the tails, on rare classes or on outliers, so the question is settled by a membership measurement, not by a loss curve.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-c2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-C2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Compare differential privacy and output coarsening as privacy controls: mechanism, guarantee and cost.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Differential privacy adds calibrated noise during training or query answering, bounding how much any single record can change an output, with composition accounting across queries; the cost is utility at useful epsilon and significant engineering effort. Output coarsening changes the interface, for example returning a band rather than a probability, which removes the fine-grained signal attacks need; the cost is product functionality and a weaker guarantee, since repeated or combined queries may still leak. DP is a property with a proof; coarsening is a reduction in information. They are complementary, and a serious design uses both.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-sc1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-SC1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A hospital wants to release a readmission model to researchers with query access. Design the privacy assessment and two controls.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The assessment measures membership inference on members and non-members drawn from the same population, reports advantage at a stated FPR, and repeats the measurement for attribute inference on a sensitive field. Controls: train with differential privacy at a stated epsilon and publish the epsilon with the utility cost, and restrict the interface to coarse risk bands with per-researcher query budgets and logging. Residual risk should be stated, because DP bounds influence rather than eliminating inference from legitimate model behaviour.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-sc2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-SC2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">An insurance app returns a fraud score to the customer. Explain the privacy exposure to the customer and to a determined attacker.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>To the customer, the score reveals how the insurer's model treats their history, which can expose inferred attributes and invite gaming. To an attacker with many queries, the score is a high-information oracle enabling extraction of the scoring function and membership tests against other people's records if identifiers can be guessed. Controls are to return a band or a reason code, rate limit per account, require step-up authentication for repeated queries, and log patterns that indicate systematic probing.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-p1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-P1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Practical programming · 8 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Implement a membership-inference evaluation: train on 200 members, score non-members, compute the ROC curve and report 2·AUC−1 plus TPR at 5 % FPR. Then repeat with a regularised model and compare.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A complete implementation splits a population into members and non-members, trains on members only, scores both groups by the probability assigned to the true label, and computes the ROC curve from concatenated scores with membership labels. It reports 2·AUC−1 and TPR at 5 % FPR via interpolation, then retrains with regularisation or fewer neighbours and repeats. The comparison is the result: leakage should fall, and the report must state that a fall is evidence about this attack, not proof of privacy. A strong answer also notes that scoring by maximum probability fails for a 1-NN model, where it is always 1.0.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-ct1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-CT1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A vendor claims their model is 'GDPR-compliant because it is anonymised'. What is wrong with the claim and what would you ask for?</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Anonymisation is a property of a dataset and its re-identification risk, not something a model can be declared to be; a model trained on personal data can leak membership and attributes even when names were removed. Ask for the legal basis for processing, the de-identification method and its measured re-identification risk, a membership and attribute-inference evaluation, the retention and deletion process including trained artefacts, and the DPIA. Compliance is a documented assessment with evidence, not an adjective attached to a model.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m09-ct2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M09-CT2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">You can add either differential privacy or query monitoring first. Argue the choice for a model that is already deployed and heavily used.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Monitoring first for a deployed, heavily used system: it can be added without retraining, it detects ongoing extraction and probing, and it produces the evidence needed to justify the utility cost of DP later. DP first if the data is highly sensitive and the model is small enough that retraining is cheap, because it bounds leakage structurally rather than observing it. The decision turns on reversibility: monitoring is reversible and immediate, DP is a training-time commitment whose epsilon must be chosen before you know how much leakage monitoring would have revealed.</p></div>
