## Multiple-choice explanations

**1. M07-Q01** — correct answer **A: Clean-label poisoning**

Clean-label attacks modify features only, so label inspection finds nothing; the signal is in the feature distribution, which is why they are the hardest to detect.

- **B** — Label flipping is defined by incorrect labels.
- **C** — Availability poisoning usually relies on wrong or noisy labels at scale.
- **D** — Relabelled triggers are the standard BadNets recipe and are visible in label statistics.

**2. M07-Q02** — correct answer **D: The poisoning rate and the clean model's trigger rate**

Attack success is meaningless without the fraction of data the attacker controlled and a baseline showing the clean model does not already produce the target label.

- **A** — Test size affects precision, not interpretation of the attack.
- **B** — Architecture details do not establish the attack's cost.
- **C** — Training time is an efficiency measure.

**3. M07-Q03** — correct answer **C: It is the attacker's access constraint and sets the cost of the attack**

Every poisoning result is conditional on how much of the training set the attacker could influence, which is what a defender can actually control through provenance and review.

- **A** — Architecture is chosen by the defender, not by the attack.
- **B** — Detection signals are measurable too, and they matter.
- **D** — Clean accuracy depends on the data and training, not on rho alone.

**4. M07-Q04** — correct answer **B: Poisoned samples form a small, coherent direction in feature space**

The defence looks for outliers along top singular directions, so it fails when an attacker spreads poison across many directions or keeps it inside the clean distribution.

- **A** — Visible patches are one attack style, not the defence's assumption.
- **C** — The defence exists precisely because the attacker can write to the training set.
- **D** — Separability is unrelated to the outlier argument.

**5. M07-Q05** — correct answer **A: Activation clustering**

Activation clustering inspects internal representations, so it needs a model; the other methods operate on the data alone.

- **B** — Label distributions are computed from the dataset.
- **C** — Duplicate analysis works on rows before training.
- **D** — Agreement statistics come from the annotation process.

**6. M07-Q06** — correct answer **D: Retraining without the poisoned rows removes the trigger reliably**

Removing the corrupted rows and retraining addresses the cause; sanitisation methods may weaken rather than remove a trigger, and none is general without knowing the trigger.

- **A** — Replacement is one option, but retraining from clean data is a repair.
- **B** — Fine-tuning can leave the trigger intact, which is why trigger sweeps are required after it.
- **C** — Pruning may or may not remove the relevant pathway and damages utility.

**7. M07-Q07** — correct answer **C: The defender's training procedure that the attacker cannot run**

The inner minimisation is the defender retraining on the poisoned data; the attacker optimises data while anticipating a training run they do not control.

- **A** — The trigger is part of the outer choice, not the inner problem.
- **B** — Evaluation is outside the bilevel formulation.
- **D** — Detection is a defender action after the fact.

**8. M07-Q08** — correct answer **B: Availability degradation across the input space**

A broad accuracy drop with no concentrated region is the signature of availability poisoning, which aims to degrade the model rather than to control a specific prediction.

- **A** — A targeted attack would show a concentrated slice failure.
- **C** — A narrow trigger leaves clean accuracy nearly unchanged.
- **D** — Clean-label attacks target specific pairs, not global accuracy.

**9. M07-Q09** — correct answer **A: Targeted and backdoor attacks concentrate their effect on a small input region**

An attack can leave the average untouched while controlling one pattern, so the evaluation must look where the attacker aimed.

- **B** — The libraries are fine; the metric is the wrong one for the question.
- **C** — Regulatory expectations vary, and this is a technical argument.
- **D** — Cost is not the reason.

**10. M07-Q10** — correct answer **D: Provenance records with per-source review and quarantine**

Poisoning enters through a data source, so attributing rows to sources and reviewing or quarantining them limits what can reach training at all.

- **A** — Validation measures the model; it does not stop the rows entering.
- **B** — Longer training lets poison influence the model more, not less.
- **C** — Architecture does not fix a corrupted dataset.

## Written model answers

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-s1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-S1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Define attack success rate for a backdoor and state the baseline it must be compared with.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Attack success rate is the fraction of triggered inputs that the model classifies as the attacker's target class. It must be compared with the same measure on a clean model, because a target class that is already common produces a high rate without any backdoor.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-s2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-S2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain in two sentences why a backdoor is described as 'a feature learned correctly'.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The trigger is perfectly predictive of the target label in the poisoned training data, so the model learns it the same way it learns any other reliable feature. Nothing in the network is malfunctioning; the corruption is in the data, which is why architectural improvements do not prevent it.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-c1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-C1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Compare spectral signatures and activation clustering as detection methods: what each assumes, what it needs, and how an adaptive attacker defeats it.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Spectral signatures assume poisoned rows form a small coherent direction in feature space; they need only the dataset and are cheap, but an attacker who spreads poison across many directions or keeps rows inside the clean distribution defeats them. Activation clustering assumes the trigger creates a separable cluster in the model's internal representation; it needs a trained model and works even when features look normal, but an attacker who makes the trigger activate similarly to clean features, or who poisons few enough rows to hide in the cluster, weakens it. Both are detection heuristics with stated assumptions, so a competent assessment names the assumption rather than claiming general detection.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-c2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-C2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain why differential privacy bounds poisoning influence in principle and why it is rarely used for that purpose in practice.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Differential privacy bounds how much any single record can change the learned parameters, which limits the influence an attacker gains from inserting rows. In practice the guarantee is expensive: useful epsilon values require noise or clipping that reduces accuracy, poisoning often works through a coordinated set of rows rather than one, and the bound is on influence per record rather than on the attacker's ability to add many records. It is therefore a useful formal argument and a weak operational control against a determined poisoning campaign.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-sc1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-SC1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A retailer fine-tunes a product classifier on images uploaded by marketplace sellers. Design a poisoning assessment and one control per pipeline stage.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Collection: seller uploads are adversarial by default, so require account history, rate limits and a per-seller quota. Ingestion: hash and store provenance per upload, scan for duplicate and edge-like patches, and review a sample. Training: cap each seller's contribution, hold out a trigger sweep and compare clean accuracy and slice metrics. Release: run a trigger search before deployment and keep the previous model for rollback. The assessment should state the realistic poisoning rate from the quota, since that number determines whether the attack is feasible at all.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-sc2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-SC2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">An admissions model was fine-tuned on a partner's historical decisions. A staff member reports that applications containing a particular reference code are always accepted. Outline your first day.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Treat it as a potential backdoor rather than a bug: preserve the model, the fine-tuning data and the code, and freeze further training. Compare acceptance rates for the flagged code against a matched control group, and test whether the code alone predicts acceptance after controlling for grades. Scan the fine-tuning rows for the code's distribution, run an ablation or feature-importance audit on the model, and check whether a clean retrain removes the effect. Report findings with numbers and a recommendation to retrain from reviewed data, and record the evidence chain because the decision affects applicants.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-p1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-P1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Practical programming · 8 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Implement a 3×3 patch trigger on an image dataset, poison 3 % of the training rows, train a small classifier, and report clean accuracy plus attack success rate against an unpoisoned baseline.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A complete implementation copies a fraction of rows, writes the patch into a fixed region, sets their labels to the target class, trains on the mixed set and measures two numbers: accuracy on clean test images and the fraction of patched test images predicted as the target. The baseline must be measured on the same patch so the report shows the clean model's natural rate. A strong answer also reports the poisoning rate and the realised patch size, and notes that a 3 % rate with an unchanged clean accuracy is the expected, and unsettling, result.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-ct1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-CT1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A vendor says their model is 'backdoor-free' because they scanned the training set with a spectral detector. Assess that claim.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The claim converts a detection result into an absence proof. A spectral detector finds a specific kind of outlier and fails against poison spread across directions, inside-distribution rows, or a trigger introduced during fine-tuning after the scan. It also says nothing about the model, which may carry a trigger from an earlier training run or from a compromised checkpoint. The defensible claim is narrower: 'no outliers of this kind were found in this snapshot by this method on this date', plus a trigger sweep on the model and a provenance record for the weights.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m07-ct2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M07-CT2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">You must choose between spending the quarter on provenance infrastructure or on detection research. Argue the choice for a company that scrapes most of its training data.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Provenance first if the corpus is scraped, because detection has no ground truth to validate against and the attack surface is the supply chain itself; source-level attribution, review and quarantine reduce exposure across all future models. Detection research is the better spend when data sources are already controlled and the residual risk is an insider or a compromised partner, where attribution exists but the rows look normal. The argument should state the assumption that flips it: if a regulator requires evidence of detection capability, research becomes mandatory regardless of provenance.</p></div>
