## Multiple-choice explanations

**1. M06-Q01** — correct answer **D: It stays within a stated budget and changes the model's decision**

Validity is defined by the constraint plus the effect: a bounded change that flips the prediction. Perceptibility is a design goal, not the definition, and transfer is a property some attacks have.

- **A** — Perceptibility is desirable but not required; many attacks are visible.
- **B** — Transfer is common but not guaranteed, so it cannot define validity.
- **C** — Training-data access is one attack setting, not a requirement.

**2. M06-Q02** — correct answer **C: It approximates the loss linearly, so it underperforms when curvature matters**

One sign step is a first-order approximation to maximising the loss; on a model with meaningful curvature, iterative steps find worse points inside the same budget.

- **A** — Iterative attacks are at least as strong at equal budget in the standard evaluation.
- **B** — FGSM needs gradients, which is why it is a white-box method.
- **D** — Detectability depends on the defence and the input, not on the attack family.

**3. M06-Q03** — correct answer **B: A transfer attack using a locally trained substitute**

A substitute model gives gradients the attacker owns, and transferability means the resulting inputs often work on the target without any query to it.

- **A** — White-box access is unavailable in the stated setting.
- **C** — Physical access is a much higher bar than query access.
- **D** — Poisoning requires pipeline access, which is stronger than query access.

**4. M06-Q04** — correct answer **A: The attacker can estimate gradients numerically or use a substitute model**

Obfuscated gradients raise the cost of one method; finite differences, score-based estimates and substitute models recover a usable direction, which is why adaptive attacks break these defences.

- **B** — Gradients matter for white-box attacks, so removing them does change an attacker's options.
- **C** — Clean accuracy is not driven to zero by masking.
- **D** — Guidance is about disclosure, not legality of a defence technique.

**5. M06-Q05** — correct answer **D: The same-budget result for random noise and the attack configuration**

Robust accuracy means nothing without the attack, its budget and a control showing that the drop comes from the attack rather than from the size of the perturbation.

- **A** — More clean data improves precision of the estimate, not its meaning.
- **B** — Clean accuracy alone does not isolate the attack's contribution.
- **C** — Capacity and training time do not describe the attack.

**6. M06-Q06** — correct answer **C: Whether that budget corresponds to an imperceptible change or a visible one**

A budget is only meaningful relative to perception and the task: 32/255 is visibly large on a photograph and may be meaningless on a feature vector with a different scale.

- **A** — Training provenance matters for leakage, not for interpreting the budget.
- **B** — Hardware does not change the perturbation size.
- **D** — Colour depth affects how the budget maps to pixels but not the central question.

**7. M06-Q07** — correct answer **B: It selects the steepest direction under an L-infinity constraint**

Under an L-infinity budget the optimal single step moves each coordinate by the full epsilon in the direction of the gradient's sign, which is what the sign operation implements.

- **A** — The normalisation is a consequence; the contribution is choosing the direction.
- **C** — Range clamping is a separate step, and sign does not perform it.
- **D** — Sign uses the gradient, so the method remains white-box.

**8. M06-Q08** — correct answer **A: Treat it as obscurity and require an adaptive evaluation**

Hidden preprocessing is a cost to the attacker, not a bound on their capability; a reviewer must ask for evaluation against attacks that model the preprocessing as part of the system.

- **B** — Proprietary detail is an assumption, and assumptions need testing.
- **C** — Publication is one option, but the requirement is an adaptive evaluation.
- **D** — Using the vendor's script tests the vendor's assumptions, not the model.

**9. M06-Q09** — correct answer **D: Both models under the same attack, budget and random restarts**

Comparisons must hold the attack fixed; otherwise the improvement may reflect a weaker evaluation rather than a more robust model.

- **A** — Different attacks make the numbers incomparable.
- **B** — Input noise without an adversary measures a different property entirely.
- **C** — Clean accuracy says nothing about robustness.

**10. M06-Q10** — correct answer **C: They learn similar features and near-linear decision structure from similar data**

Transfer works because the loss surface has directions that are adversarial for many models trained on similar data, which is why a substitute can be used as a proxy.

- **A** — Seeds are irrelevant to transfer and are rarely shared.
- **B** — Transfer occurs across architectures, which is the point of the technique.
- **D** — Gradients differ between models; similarity, not identity, is what transfers.

## Written model answers

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-s1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-S1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Define an adversarial example formally in one sentence, naming the constraint and the objective.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>An adversarial example is an input x' within a stated distance of a legitimate input x, such as ||x' - x|| ≤ epsilon, that causes the model to produce a chosen wrong output while x is classified correctly.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-s2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-S2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain in two sentences why a robustness number must state its norm.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Different norms describe different kinds of change: L-infinity bounds every pixel, while L2 allows a few large changes and many small ones. A model can be robust in one norm and fragile in another, so a number without a norm cannot be compared with any other result.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-c1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-C1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain why an adversarially trained model is not 'secure', using the concepts of budget, attack family and adaptivity.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Adversarial training minimises loss on inputs perturbed within one budget under one attack family, so it buys measured resistance to that configuration. An attacker who uses a larger budget, a different norm, a physical transformation or a different objective is outside the training distribution, and an adaptive attacker specifically searches for the region the defence did not cover. The trained model is therefore more robust in a defined sense, and the claim must state the sense: which norm, which budget, which attack and what measured accuracy.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-c2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-C2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A defence reports 90 % accuracy under attack. List three reasons that number could be an artefact of the evaluation rather than evidence of robustness.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>First, the attack may have been too weak: too few steps, no random restarts, or gradient masking that defeats the specific optimiser. Second, the budget may have been smaller than the threat model assumes, so the attack was not allowed to do what an adversary could. Third, the evaluation may have used a different preprocessing path from production, or a model in eval mode with different behaviour. Each is fixed by a stronger evaluation, not by a better claim.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-sc1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-SC1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A bank deploys a cheque-image classifier. Describe a white-box, a transfer and a physical attack, and rank them by the access each requires.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>White-box: the attacker has the weights and optimises a bounded perturbation, the cheapest attack given that access. Transfer: the attacker trains a substitute on collected cheque images, generates perturbations there and submits them, requiring data and query access but no weights. Physical: the attacker prints a sticker or alters the cheque so the camera captures the perturbation, requiring the most effort and surviving compression and lighting only with careful design. Ranking by access: white-box highest access, then physical (repeatable but constrained), then transfer, which is the most practical remote threat.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-sc2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-SC2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A team wants to ship an 'adversarially robust' spam filter. Specify the evaluation you would require before accepting the claim.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>State the threat model: text transformations a spammer can apply, such as homoglyphs, invisible characters, synonym substitution and appended tokens, with the constraint that the message stays readable. Evaluate with several attacks at matched budgets, including a black-box query attack and a human-authored set, and report clean accuracy, attacked accuracy and a random-perturbation control. Require the results on a held-out set not used for training the defence, with seeds and variance. The claim is then a sentence with numbers, not the word 'robust'.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-p1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-P1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Practical programming · 8 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Implement `fgsm(model, x, y, eps)` for a small classifier, verify that the realised L-infinity change equals eps, and report clean and attacked accuracy on 200 test inputs.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A complete implementation computes the loss gradient with respect to the input, takes its sign, multiplies by eps, adds and clamps to the valid range, and returns a detached tensor. Verification takes the maximum absolute difference between original and adversarial input and asserts it is within eps with a small tolerance; clamping can reduce the realised change, which is why the check is an upper bound rather than equality. Reporting should include clean accuracy, attacked accuracy and the number of predictions that flipped, because the flip count separates attacks that shift confidence from those that change decisions.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-ct1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-CT1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">An engineer argues that adversarial examples do not matter because a human would never make that change. Construct the strongest counter-argument and the strongest concession.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The counter-argument is that the threat model is the attacker, not the average user: an adversary chooses inputs deliberately, and the existence of a cheap bounded input that flips the decision is a real capability. The same perturbations also arise accidentally from compression, sensor noise and benign distribution shift, so robustness is not only an attack property. The concession is that imperceptible perturbations to images have limited impact in domains where inputs are already noisy and reviewed, so the priority should follow consequence: it matters most where decisions are automated and the attacker gains.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m06-ct2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M06-CT2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">You have one week and one GPU. Choose between adversarial training and a stronger evaluation of the existing model, and defend the choice.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Choose the evaluation if the current robustness claim is unverified, because a stronger attack can invalidate a defence that was never tested adaptively, and shipping an unverified claim is the larger risk. Choose adversarial training if the evaluation already exists and shows a specific weakness the product must tolerate. The defence of either choice rests on what is unknown: evaluation reduces uncertainty about the current model, training changes the model. If the deadline is immovable and the claim is public, evaluation first is the defensible order.</p></div>
