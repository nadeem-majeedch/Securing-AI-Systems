## Multiple-choice explanations

**1. M08-Q01** — correct answer **B: That the perturbation stays inside the allowed distance from the input**

Without projection, iterative ascent leaves the budget on the first or second step and the reported robustness describes an unbounded attack.

- **A** — Success depends on the model and budget, not on projection.
- **C** — Perceptual validity is a separate concern from the norm constraint.
- **D** — Projection is a geometric operation and does not create gradients.

**2. M08-Q02** — correct answer **A: The smoothed classifier's prediction is unchanged within an L2 ball of that radius**

The certificate concerns the smoothed predictor under L2 perturbation and says nothing about whether the prediction is correct, nor about other norms or attack families.

- **B** — The guarantee applies to the smoothed classifier, not the base model.
- **C** — Other norms and attack families are explicitly outside the certificate.
- **D** — Accuracy and radius are different quantities entirely.

**3. M08-Q03** — correct answer **D: The PGD evaluation was defeated by gradient masking or a weak configuration**

When a different attack family succeeds where the tuned one fails, the original evaluation was probably measuring the attack's weakness rather than the model's robustness.

- **A** — A defence that fails to a simple attack is not sound.
- **B** — Retraining does not address an evaluation error.
- **C** — Transfer is a standard and cheap threat model.

**4. M08-Q04** — correct answer **C: As both numbers, because the gain was bought with clean utility**

A defence is a trade, and the report is the pair of numbers plus the attack configuration; reporting only the favourable one is how model cards mislead.

- **A** — Tripling a low number without the cost is an incomplete report.
- **B** — A small clean drop can be an acceptable trade, so 'failure' overstates it.
- **D** — The result is informative, not inconclusive.

**5. M08-Q05** — correct answer **B: To avoid reporting robustness that only holds from one starting point**

Gradient ascent is local, so a single start can miss a failure region; restarts make the estimate closer to the worst case the budget allows.

- **A** — Query cost is a side effect, not the purpose.
- **C** — Restarts add cost rather than reducing it.
- **D** — Restarts improve coverage but do not guarantee optimality.

**6. M08-Q06** — correct answer **A: 'Under an L2 budget of 0.5, PGD-20 with 5 restarts reduced accuracy to 0.71.'**

It states norm, budget, attack, configuration and measured outcome, which is exactly what a reader needs to judge and reproduce the result.

- **B** — 'All known attacks' is unverifiable and invites contradiction.
- **C** — No attack, budget or result is specified.
- **D** — An unnamed team and method cannot be checked.

**7. M08-Q07** — correct answer **D: It bounds behaviour for every input in a region, not just tested points**

A certificate quantifies over a neighbourhood, so it cannot be defeated by finding one more adversarial input inside the ball; empirical results are samples of an unknown worst case.

- **A** — Certification is usually more expensive.
- **B** — Accuracy is not implied by a certificate.
- **C** — Certificates are norm-specific in practice.

**8. M08-Q08** — correct answer **C: An adaptive attack that differentiates through the randomised preprocessing**

Random preprocessing is a classic gradient-masking technique; the only way to know whether it helps is to attack the composed system, including the randomness.

- **A** — Default PGD may fail against masking and say nothing about the defence.
- **B** — Clean accuracy does not test robustness.
- **D** — A baseline comparison does not evaluate the defence under attack.

**9. M08-Q09** — correct answer **B: Report the trade, offer a cheaper partial defence and state the residual risk**

Constraints are part of the problem: the useful contribution is a costed option plus an explicit statement of what remains exposed, which the owner can accept or reject.

- **A** — Ignoring a hard product constraint produces a defence nobody ships.
- **C** — Deleting a risk from the register does not remove it.
- **D** — Hiding a budget change defers the decision to an incident.

**10. M08-Q10** — correct answer **A: Step size and number of steps**

Step size and iteration count determine how far the attack can travel inside the budget and how well it optimises the loss; both must be reported.

- **B** — Those are training parameters.
- **C** — Those are regularisation settings.
- **D** — Those describe the task, not the optimiser.

## Written model answers

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-s1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-S1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain in two sentences why an unprojected attack produces a meaningless robustness number.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Without projection the perturbation is no longer bounded by the declared epsilon, so the attack is solving an easier problem than the one in the threat model. The resulting accuracy describes a model under an unbounded or much larger change, which cannot be compared with any other published result.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-s2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-S2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">State the difference between robust accuracy and certified accuracy.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Robust accuracy is the measured accuracy under a specific attack at a specific budget. Certified accuracy is the fraction of inputs for which the prediction is provably unchanged within a radius, and it is usually lower because it counts only inputs the certificate covers.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-c1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-C1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain gradient masking, name two ways it can be introduced accidentally, and say how an evaluator detects it.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Gradient masking occurs when the gradients an attacker can compute are uninformative, so gradient-based attacks understate the model's fragility. It arises accidentally from randomised preprocessing, from non-differentiable operations such as quantisation or argmax, and from defences that deliberately shatter gradients. An evaluator detects it by comparing attacks: if a black-box or transfer attack succeeds where PGD fails, the gradients are misleading. The remedy is adaptive evaluation with several attack families and a documented configuration, not a stronger claim about the defence.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-c2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-C2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain the robustness-utility trade-off using the numbers from an adversarial-training run, and why the trade cannot be assumed away.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Adversarial training changes the objective to include worst-case inputs inside a budget, which typically lowers clean accuracy and costs training compute: a run that raises robust accuracy from 0.07 to 0.23 may lower clean accuracy from 0.84 to 0.79. The trade is structural, because robustness requires the decision boundary to move away from training points, which reduces fit to the clean distribution. It cannot be assumed away by more data or longer training alone, and any claim that both improved must be shown with matched attacks and a held-out set.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-sc1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-SC1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A medical imaging product claims robustness to 'small perturbations'. Design the evaluation that would let the claim be accepted, naming the four things you need.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>First, a threat model with a norm and a budget justified by acquisition and compression effects, not by convenience. Second, an attack suite: PGD with restarts, a transfer attack from a substitute, and a physical or acquisition-realistic transform, each at the stated budget. Third, a control at the same budget, such as random noise, to separate attack effect from general degradation. Fourth, held-out data and reported clean accuracy alongside attacked accuracy, with seeds and variance. Acceptance requires the claim to be rewritten to match what those numbers support.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-sc2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-SC2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A team's defence is 'input sanitisation': it resizes, compresses and quantises images before classification. Evaluate the security value of that choice.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Sanitisation removes some high-frequency perturbations and raises the cost of naive attacks, which is real but incidental robustness. It is not a bound: an attacker who models the transform inside the optimisation can produce perturbations that survive resizing and compression, and quantisation can mask gradients, making evaluations look better than the model is. The security value depends on whether the pipeline is attacked end to end in evaluation; without that, sanitisation is an operational hygiene measure that should not appear in a robustness claim.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-p1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-P1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Practical programming · 8 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Implement PGD with a random start and projection, assert the realised budget for epsilon values 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2, and report accuracy under FGSM and PGD at each budget.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A complete implementation starts from a uniform random point inside the epsilon ball, takes sign-gradient steps of size alpha, projects onto the ball after each step, and clamps to the valid input range. The budget assertion compares the maximum absolute difference to epsilon and fails if it exceeds it. The report should show accuracy for both attacks at all three budgets, which usually reveals that the iterative attack is at least as strong and that the gap widens with budget. A strong answer notes that the realised change can be slightly below epsilon because of clamping.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-ct1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-CT1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A paper reports 80 % robust accuracy. Reproduce the three questions you would ask before citing it, and why each matters.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Ask which attack and configuration produced the number, because a weak attack inflates it. Ask what budget and norm were used, because robustness at a small budget does not transfer to a realistic threat. Ask whether the evaluation was adaptive to the defence and whether code and seeds are available, because published defences frequently fall to attacks designed after the fact. Each question targets a way the number can be true and irrelevant, which is the failure mode that matters when citing work in a risk assessment.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m08-ct2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M08-CT2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A regulator wants 'robustness testing' before approval of a biometric system. Draft the standard you would propose, including what it must not allow.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The standard should require a stated threat model covering digital and presentation attacks with justified budgets, a fixed attack suite including adaptive and transfer attacks, reporting of clean and attacked error rates with confidence intervals, a same-budget control, and disclosure of the defence's training objective. It must not allow self-selected attacks, undisclosed preprocessing, single-seed results, or a pass mark expressed as a single accuracy without a false-accept and false-reject breakdown. The rationale is that robustness is a measurement relative to a threat, so the standard's job is to fix the threat and the reporting, not the model.</p></div>
