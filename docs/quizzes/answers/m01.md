## Multiple-choice explanations

**1. M01-Q01** — correct answer **C: Security covers deliberate adversaries; safety covers unintended harm.**

The distinguishing feature is intent: security assumes someone chooses inputs to make the system fail, while safety analyses accidents under expected use.

- **A** — Both fields span the whole lifecycle, so the split is intent rather than phase.
- **B** — This is close but understates the distinction: security is defined by the presence of an adversary seeking failure, not by a measurement of resistance, while safety covers accidents in normal use.
- **D** — This inverts the relationship: the two are complementary analyses of different causes.

**2. M01-Q02** — correct answer **B: The index and its access control**

The index is treated as an ordinary database and inherits protections it does not have, which is why cross-tenant retrieval keeps appearing as a finding in production reviews.

- **A** — Weights are the first component most teams consider, and supply-chain checks now cover them.
- **C** — Training data is prominent in every discussion of poisoning and privacy.
- **D** — API authentication is the most familiar control in the stack and rarely omitted.

**3. M01-Q03** — correct answer **A: Accuracy hides behaviour in the small input region an attacker chooses.**

An attacker does not sample from the test distribution: a model can be 97 % accurate overall and wrong on every input in a crafted region.

- **B** — Leakage is a real risk, but the decisive problem is that the average is the wrong statistic for an adversary.
- **C** — No accuracy threshold makes a model safe; an attack does not care about the average.
- **D** — Fairness matters, but it is not what makes accuracy inadequate as a security measure.

**4. M01-Q04** — correct answer **D: An accidental distribution shift**

Nobody chose the input to cause harm, but the effect on customers matches an attack, which is why accidental and adversarial failures share a monitoring path.

- **A** — Evasion requires an adversary crafting inputs, and none is present here.
- **B** — Poisoning corrupts training data, and the training data has not changed.
- **C** — Extraction copies the model, and nothing is being copied.

**5. M01-Q05** — correct answer **C: Validate input and authorise at the service boundary**

Boundary controls cut injection, malformed input, cross-tenant access and tool abuse at once, while model-level defences are narrower and can be undone by an adaptive attacker.

- **A** — Adversarial training helps against perturbation attacks and little else, at a utility cost.
- **B** — A larger evaluation set improves measurement, not resistance.
- **D** — A model card is transparency, not a control; it changes no attacker's options.

**6. M01-Q06** — correct answer **B: Insiders and stolen credentials reach internal services too.**

The perimeter limits who can reach the service, not what they can do once inside; agents and integrations routinely forward untrusted text to internal endpoints.

- **A** — Encryption in transit is orthogonal: the objection is about reachability, not confidentiality.
- **C** — Rate limiting is a control the team can add, not a property internal APIs lack.
- **D** — Weight theft is possible but is a narrower argument than the one that defeats the claim.

**7. M01-Q07** — correct answer **A: Poisoning at training; injection at inference**

Poisoning corrupts the data a model learns from, while injection manipulates text at request time; placing a threat correctly is what makes a control possible at all.

- **B** — Extraction happens at inference, and evasion also happens at inference.
- **C** — This reverses both threats.
- **D** — A backdoor is installed during training, and membership inference exploits a trained model.

**8. M01-Q08** — correct answer **D: Which attack was run, at what budget, with what result?**

A security claim is an attack configuration plus a measured outcome; without both, the other answers describe capacity and operations rather than resistance.

- **A** — Parameter count is a rough capacity proxy and says nothing about resistance.
- **B** — Region affects residency and latency, not behaviour under attack.
- **C** — Dataset size is a quality signal, not evidence about adversarial behaviour.

**9. M01-Q09** — correct answer **C: A structural fix to the capability it reached**

The payload is one instance of a class; the regression test is useful, but without constraining the capability the next phrasing succeeds.

- **A** — That is the measure the team already took: it stops one exact string and leaves the capability reachable by any paraphrase of it.
- **B** — Model size is irrelevant to whether a capability can be invoked.
- **D** — Raising a threshold hides the signal instead of removing the cause.

**10. M01-Q10** — correct answer **B: Containment and evidence preservation**

Detection without containment lets the incident continue, and without preserved evidence you cannot establish what happened before you rebuild.

- **A** — Retraining is part of recovery, and doing it first destroys the evidence.
- **C** — Reporting follows understanding, not detection.
- **D** — Threshold changes need the evidence you have not collected yet.

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-s1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-S1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">In two sentences, explain why an AI system's attack surface is larger than its model's input tensor.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The model is one component of a pipeline: collection, retrieval, prompt assembly, tool calls, credentials and the serving API are all reachable and all change what the model sees. An attack can therefore succeed without a formally adversarial input, for example by poisoning a retrieved document or by invoking a tool with the model's authority.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-s2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-S2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">State the difference between a vulnerability and a risk in one sentence each, using an AI example.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A vulnerability is a weakness that can be exploited: the retrieval endpoint does not check tenant, so any authenticated user can read another tenant's document. A risk is that weakness combined with likelihood and impact in a deployment: a support assistant indexing client contracts makes it a likely breach, while an empty index leaves it latent.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-c1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-C1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A colleague claims a model with 99 % benchmark accuracy is 'secure enough'. Give three distinct reasons the claim fails, and the evidence that would replace it.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>First, the benchmark distribution is not the attacker's distribution, so the average says nothing about a crafted input region. Second, accuracy measures the model alone while attacks also target data, retrieval, prompts, tools and credentials. Third, accuracy is a static snapshot against an adaptive adversary. Replace it with an attack-and-control record: named attacks, budgets, measured success rates, a same-budget control, and an explicit statement of what remains untested.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-c2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-C2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain why security is a property of a deployment rather than of a model, using one model deployed twice.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>The same weights can be safe in one deployment and dangerous in another because the surrounding controls differ. A classifier that returns only a label, behind authentication, with per-tenant quotas and no tool access exposes little. The identical classifier behind an unauthenticated endpoint returning probabilities and able to trigger an email exposes extraction, privacy and injection risk. The model supplies capability; the deployment supplies the attack surface, the trust boundaries and the consequences.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-sc1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-SC1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A university assistant answers admissions questions from an index and can email a summary to an applicant. The index holds documents for three faculties. Name the four most important trust boundaries and one control for each.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Applicant to assistant (untrusted text reaches the model): constrain tools by allowlist and validate arguments. Assistant to index (retrieval scope): filter candidates by faculty and role before ranking. Assistant to email (outbound capability): fixed recipient domain, no free-form addresses, human confirmation. Operator to index (content authorship): provenance checks and review before ingestion. A strong answer adds that the model's own instructions are not a boundary.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-sc2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-SC2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">An insurance pricing model degrades after an upstream change to income formatting. Describe how you would distinguish this from a deliberate attack within the first hour.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Compare feature-distribution change against deployment events: a schema change timestamped before the degradation points to an accident. Check request provenance for concentrated sources or crafted patterns, and compare against a canary set of known-good inputs. Look for a step change rather than gradual drift, and ask whether outputs moved in a direction that benefits someone. Preserve logs in both cases, because the first hour's evidence decides whether this becomes an incident or a rollback.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-p1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-P1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Practical programming · 8 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Write `surface(model_card: dict) -&gt; list[dict]` that turns a model card plus a deployment description into attack-surface entries with `asset`, `entry_point`, `threat` and `control`. Cover at least six entries for a RAG assistant with an email tool, and assert that no entry has an empty control.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A complete answer enumerates assets (weights, training data, index, prompt template, credentials, tool APIs, logs), maps each to an entry point (upload, ingestion job, request body, tool call, key store, log sink), names a threat per entry (poisoning, cross-tenant retrieval, injection, escalation, theft, tampering) and pairs each with a control (hash manifest, ACL before ranking, allowlist, least-privilege token, encryption, append-only audit). The assertion iterates the list and fails on an empty or placeholder control; a strong answer also fails on duplicate `(asset, entry_point)` pairs, because duplicates usually mean padding rather than analysis.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-ct1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-CT1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A model card claims 'robust to adversarial input' and you must approve the claim. What makes you reject it, and what wording would you accept?</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Reject it when no attack, norm, budget or measured success rate is stated; when the evaluation used a weaker attack than the one used in training; when there is no same-budget control; or when the claim spans modalities and norms. Accept wording such as: 'under an L-infinity budget of 8/255, PGD-20 reduced accuracy from 0.91 to 0.74 on this evaluation set; no claim is made for other norms, physical perturbations or adaptive attacks.' A claim is a measurement plus its scope and limits, not an adjective.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m01-ct2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M01-CT2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">You have budget for either a red-team exercise or a monitoring platform this year, not both. Argue for one and state the assumption that would reverse your choice.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Take monitoring if the system is already deployed and changing: incidents recur, and detection plus response bounds their duration, whereas a one-off exercise expires as soon as the model or data changes. Take red teaming if the system is pre-launch and nothing is known about its failure modes, because monitoring cannot alert on behaviour nobody has characterised. The choice reverses if the deployment is stable and already instrumented, or if a regulator requires independent testing evidence: then the marginal value of another alert is lower than that of an adversarial assessment.</p></div>
