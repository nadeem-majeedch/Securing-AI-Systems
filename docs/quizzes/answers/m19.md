## Multiple-choice explanations

**1. M19-Q01** — correct answer **D: Obligations proportionate to the risk class of the intended use**

Risk-based regimes tier obligations: transparency and documentation broadly, conformity assessment and stricter controls for high-risk uses, prohibitions for a small set.

- **A** — Uniform obligations are the opposite of a risk-based approach.
- **B** — Certification varies by regime and is rarely a single global body.
- **C** — Training data publication is not generally required and often impossible.

**2. M19-Q02** — correct answer **C: Purpose limitation**

Purpose limitation asks whether the new use is compatible with the purpose for which the data was collected, which is the threshold question before any technical control.

- **A** — Interpretability matters later in the assessment.
- **B** — Storage efficiency is an operational concern.
- **D** — Latency is unrelated.

**3. M19-Q03** — correct answer **B: Nobody is answerable for the system's behaviour or for acting on its risks**

Accountability is a person or role with authority to decide and be answerable; documentation supports that role but cannot substitute for it.

- **A** — Documentation describes; it does not decide.
- **C** — Length is not the issue.
- **D** — Interpretability is a separate property.

**4. M19-Q04** — correct answer **A: The reviewer has the information, time, authority and competence to reverse the decision**

Oversight is real when the person can understand the recommendation, has time to disagree and can change the outcome without penalty.

- **B** — Click-through approval at volume becomes rubber-stamping.
- **C** — A log entry proves presence, not judgement.
- **D** — Annual review is not oversight of individual decisions.

**5. M19-Q05** — correct answer **D: A documented threat model with owners and residual risk**

A threat model with owners and residual risk serves both the assurance case and day-to-day decisions about what to fix next.

- **A** — Marketing material is not assurance evidence.
- **B** — Financial history is irrelevant.
- **C** — Hyperparameters are insufficient and rarely decision-relevant.

**6. M19-Q06** — correct answer **C: It states the evidence, the decision-maker and the assumption that would defeat it**

Acceptability is a decision that cites evidence and names its own fragility; a signature without those elements is authority without reasoning.

- **A** — Seniority does not supply the argument.
- **B** — A template is formatting.
- **D** — Zero residual risk is not credible.

**7. M19-Q07** — correct answer **B: A structured analysis of necessity, proportionality and risks to individuals' rights**

A DPIA focuses on whether the processing is necessary and proportionate and on the specific harms to individuals, including mitigations and consultation.

- **A** — Cost is not its purpose.
- **C** — Marketing is unrelated.
- **D** — Competitor analysis is not part of it.

**8. M19-Q08** — correct answer **A: A clinician-visible risk with the evidence behind it and authority to ignore it**

Oversight requires that the clinician can see why the flag appeared and can overrule it; automation removes the judgement the tool is meant to support.

- **B** — Automatic orders remove human judgement from a consequential decision.
- **C** — Accuracy auditing is retrospective and does not oversee individual cases.
- **D** — Suppression hides information rather than supporting a decision.

**9. M19-Q09** — correct answer **D: The provider's behaviour, changes and data handling become part of your system's risk**

The provider's model changes, retention and incident handling affect your obligations, so contractual and monitoring controls are part of your governance.

- **A** — API security depends on design, not on being an API.
- **B** — A DPIA remains necessary.
- **C** — Third-party models can be evaluated behaviourally.

**10. M19-Q10** — correct answer **C: Transparency must be aimed at the audience that needs to act, and explain decisions in their terms**

Transparency is a communication obligation to affected people and operators, and its test is whether they can understand and act, not whether a document exists.

- **A** — A model card is one artefact among several obligations.
- **B** — Weights are not usually required for transparency.
- **D** — Affected individuals are central, not only regulators.

Documentation records what was decided and why, and supports accountability without replacing the person who must act.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-s2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-S2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Short answer · 3 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Name two conditions that make human oversight of an AI recommendation ineffective and explain why.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Volume: if a reviewer must approve hundreds of outputs per hour, approval becomes a click rather than an assessment. Information asymmetry: if the reviewer cannot see the evidence behind a recommendation, they cannot form an independent judgement and will defer to the system.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-c1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-C1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Explain why compliance with a regulation does not establish that a system is secure, using two concrete examples.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Regulation specifies obligations and evidence, while security is a property of a deployment under an adversary. A system can satisfy documentation, transparency and impact-assessment duties and still have a retrieval bug that exposes one tenant to another, because no clause tests isolation. It can also have a complete risk register whose residual risk is accepted on an assumption that a tester would falsify in an hour. Compliance makes the organisation answerable and auditable; security requires adversarial evidence such as an ACL suite, a measured attack success rate and a recovery test.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-c2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-C2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Conceptual · 5 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A public-sector body wants to deploy a risk-scoring tool for benefit eligibility. Set out the governance questions that must be answered before any technical work.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Is the use lawful and proportionate, and has a DPIA been completed with the affected population consulted? Who is accountable for decisions, and can a person contest an outcome with a human who has authority to change it? What is the legal basis for the data, and how will accuracy and disparate impact be measured across groups before and after deployment? What is published to applicants, in what language, and what is the review cycle? These are prior questions because they determine whether the system may exist at all, not merely how it should be built.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-sc1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-SC1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A hospital deploys a triage assistant that drafts summaries for clinicians. Produce the governance pack's four essential components and one failure each prevents.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A DPIA covering purpose, necessity, data flows and risks to patients, preventing lawful-but-harmful processing. A named clinical owner with authority to suspend the tool, preventing an unowned system drifting into use. A human-oversight design showing what the clinician sees and how they overrule, preventing automation bias. A monitoring and incident plan with review dates, preventing a silent degradation. Each component answers a question a regulator or a coroner would ask, which is why the pack is written for that reader rather than for the engineering team.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-sc2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-SC2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Scenario-based · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">A vendor's model is used in a hiring pipeline. Describe the contractual and monitoring controls you would require.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Contractually: a defined purpose, prohibition on reuse for other clients, notification of model changes, audit rights, breach notification timelines, deletion on termination and evidence of bias testing. Operationally: a fixed model version with digests, quarterly disparate-impact measurement on your own applicant pool, a human review path for rejected candidates, and logging sufficient to reconstruct any decision. The controls matter because the vendor's changes and data handling become part of your risk, and you remain answerable to applicants.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-p1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-P1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Practical programming · 8 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">Write `assess(system: dict) -&gt; dict` that returns a governance checklist with pass, gap or unknown per item, and a blocking flag for missing accountability or DPIA on personal data.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>A complete implementation defines items such as named owner, DPIA for personal data, published transparency notice, oversight design, retention schedule and incident contact, and evaluates each from the input dictionary. Missing values are reported as 'unknown' rather than 'pass', which is the honest default. Blocking is set when the owner is absent or when personal data is processed without a DPIA, and the returned structure includes the reason for each block. A strong answer separates 'not applicable' from 'unknown' so the report cannot be gamed by omission.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-ct1-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-CT1</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">'We comply with the AI Act, therefore our system is trustworthy.' Assess this claim.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Compliance demonstrates that specified obligations were met for a defined intended use; trustworthiness also depends on behaviour under adversarial conditions and on whether the specified obligations capture the harms that actually occur. A conformity assessment can pass while cross-tenant retrieval is untested, because the regime asks for risk management processes rather than for a specific isolation test. The defensible claim is narrower: the system meets these obligations, with this evidence, and these residual risks are accepted by this role. Trust is a judgement the reader makes from that record, not a status conferred by a file.</p></div>

<div class="sas-answer" id="m19-ct2-answer"><p class="sas-item__head"><strong>M19-CT2</strong> <span class="sas-meta">Critical thinking · 6 marks</span></p><p class="sas-answer__q">You must decide whether to publish a system card that reveals a known limitation. Argue both sides and recommend.</p><p class="sas-answer__label">Model answer</p><p>Publishing the limitation lets affected users and auditors calibrate their reliance, supports contestability, and reduces the chance that the limitation becomes a concealed defect discovered later with worse consequences. Withholding protects competitive information and avoids misleading users about a limitation that may be rare in practice, but it transfers risk to people who cannot see it and creates a documentation gap that a regulator will treat as aggravating. Recommend publishing the limitation with its measured frequency, impact and mitigation, and withholding only the implementation detail that would enable abuse.</p></div>
